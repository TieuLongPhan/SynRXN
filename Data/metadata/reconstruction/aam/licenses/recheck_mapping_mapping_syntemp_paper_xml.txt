center
as well as the surrounding context in order to specify salient features
of chemical reactions. They are subgraphs of <italic>imaginary transition
states</italic>, which are equivalent to double pushout graph rewriting
rules and thus can be applied directly to predict reaction outcomes
at the structural formula level. We introduce here <monospace>SynTemp</monospace>, a framework designed to extract and hierarchically cluster reaction
templates from large-scale reaction data repositories. Rule inference
is implemented as a robust graph-theoretic approach, which first computes
an atom–atom mapping (AAM) as a consensus over partial predictions
from multiple state-of-the-art tools and then augments the raw AAM
by mechanistically relevant hydrogen atoms and extracts the reactions
center extended by relevant context. <monospace>SynTemp</monospace> achieves an exceptional accuracy of 99.5% and a success rate of
71.23% in obtaining AAMs on the <italic>chemical reaction dataset</italic>. Hierarchical clustering of the extended reaction centers based
on topological features results in a library of 311 transformation
rules explaining 86% of the reaction dataset.</p></abstract><custom-meta-group><custom-meta><meta-name>status</meta-name><meta-value>released</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>display-pdf</meta-name><meta-value>yes</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-in-collection-domain</meta-name><meta-value>yes</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-olf</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-manuscript</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-preprint</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-journal-matter</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-scanned</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-retracted</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta><notes notes-type="article-notes"><sec id="historyarticle-meta1" sec-type="history" disp-level="2"><p>Received 2024 Sep 30; Accepted 2025 Feb 17; Revised 2025 Feb 12; Collection date 2025 Mar 24.</p></sec></notes></front><body><sec id="sec1" disp-level="1"><label>1.</label><title>Introduction</title><p>Recent advancements in
Artificial Intelligence (AI) and data-driven
methods have markedly accelerated the molecular design of drug candidates
and materials. The development of recipes to construct a target compound
from available starting materials by orchestrating a sequence of chemically
viable reaction steps, i.e., <italic>synthesis planning</italic>,
thus also is increasingly treated as a computational problem. Traditionally,
it is most commonly approached by retrosynthetic analysis, formalized
by E. J. Corey<sup><xref rid="ref1" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>,<xref rid="ref2" ref-type="bibr">2</xref></sup> in the 1960s as the recursive
decomposition of the product considering the reverse of synthetic
reactions. This calls for the exploration of a vast array of potential
chemical reactions that have not yet been observed for a specific
set of reactants but are analogous to known chemical transformations.
Modern synthesis planning therefore crucially relies on <italic>reaction
patterns</italic> that summarize the accumulated knowledge about feasible
chemical reactions. Here, we describe a computational framework, <monospace>SynTemp</monospace>, designed to extract such reaction patterns from
large-scale repositories of chemical reactions and to make them available
in the form of explicit transformation rules.</p><p>Significant data
resources for chemical reactions include the United
States Patent and Trademark Office (<monospace>USPTO</monospace>)
database<sup><xref rid="ref3" ref-type="bibr">3</xref></sup> and the commercial platform <monospace>Reaxys</monospace>.<sup><xref rid="ref4" ref-type="bibr">4</xref></sup> In particular, the
latter, with its collection of over 55 million manually curated reactions,
has become a pivotal resource for implementing deep learning models
in retrosynthesis.<sup><xref rid="ref5" ref-type="bibr">5</xref></sup> Efficient synthesis
planning for novel compounds, by its very nature, requires reasoning
by analogy. This can be greatly facilitated by a representation of
reactions that provides transformation rules applicable to novel compounds
and novel combinations of reactants. A very transparent, explicit
model of chemical reactions is provided by graph grammars, implemented,
e.g., in <monospace>MØD</monospace>.<sup><xref rid="ref6" ref-type="bibr">6</xref></sup> Here, molecules are encoded as graphs and reactions are defined
by transformation rules that rewrite local patterns of chemical bonds.<sup><xref rid="ref7" ref-type="bibr">7</xref></sup> Specific transformation rules, e.g., for well-known <italic>named reactions</italic>, are not challenging to write manually;
for instance, <monospace>Synthia</monospace> (or <monospace>Chematica</monospace>(<xref rid="ref8" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>)) successfully applies this approach,
in its complex synthesis planning utilizing a database of approximately
70,000 expert-encoded rules.<sup><xref rid="ref9" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>,<xref rid="ref10" ref-type="bibr">10</xref></sup> This level of effort,
however, is unlikely to scale to the entirety of present-day chemical
knowledge<sup><xref rid="ref5" ref-type="bibr">5</xref></sup> and cannot keep pace with the
continuing exponential increase in the number of reported reactions.<sup><xref rid="ref11" ref-type="bibr">11</xref></sup></p><p>Hence, automatic methods to mine comprehensive
data repositories
for transformation rules are urgently needed. Recent efforts in this
direction include the study by Shuan Chen et al.,<sup><xref rid="ref12" ref-type="bibr">12</xref></sup> which utilizes a generalized-template-based graph neural
network, and research by Lung-Yi Chen,<sup><xref rid="ref13" ref-type="bibr">13</xref></sup> focusing on extraction and curation of reaction templates. <monospace>SynTemp</monospace> aims to produce explicit, interpretable patterns.
The graph-theoretical approach pursued here yields a representation
that not only has an equivalent interpretation as a computationally
operational reaction rule but also can form the basis for a mechanistic
reaction classification.</p><p>Reaction rules, by definition, describe
bond changes and thus establish
a one-to-one mapping between the atoms of the reactants and the products
in the reaction center. The inference of such rules, therefore, requires
reaction data endowed with atom–atom maps (AAMs). Sizable repositories
of reaction data, such as <monospace>Reaxys</monospace>(<xref rid="ref4" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>) or the United States Patent and Trademark Office
(<monospace>USPTO</monospace>) database,<sup><xref rid="ref3" ref-type="bibr">3</xref></sup> however, provide reaction data only as sets of reactant and product
molecules without AAM information. AAM inference, therefore, is the
crucial first step toward accurate reaction patterns.</p><p>Methods
for computing AAMs can be divided into rule-based and machine
learning-based (ML-based) approaches. Rule-based techniques, such
as <monospace>Automapper</monospace>,<sup><xref rid="ref14" ref-type="bibr">14</xref></sup><monospace>Indigo</monospace>,<sup><xref rid="ref15" ref-type="bibr">15</xref></sup><monospace>NameRXN</monospace>,<sup><xref rid="ref16" ref-type="bibr">16</xref></sup><monospace>RDTool</monospace>,<sup><xref rid="ref17" ref-type="bibr">17</xref></sup> and a complex tool described by Jaworski,<sup><xref rid="ref18" ref-type="bibr">18</xref></sup> rely on combinatorial optimization criteria
such as minimal chemical distance (MCD)<sup><xref rid="ref19" ref-type="bibr">19</xref></sup> or maximum-common subgraph (MCS).<sup><xref rid="ref20" ref-type="bibr">20</xref></sup> These
rule-based solutions face challenges in practice. Most importantly,
the optimization objectives of MCD and MCS only approximate the inference
of the actual chemical mechanism. Therefore, even exact solutions
of the corresponding combinatorial optimization problem may yield
a chemically incorrect AAM.<sup><xref rid="ref18" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>,<xref rid="ref21" ref-type="bibr">21</xref></sup> Computational cost,
moreover, may become an issue since various variants of MCS are well-known
NP-complete problems.<sup><xref rid="ref22" ref-type="bibr">22</xref></sup> As an alternative,
several ML-based tools have become available in recent years, most
prominently <monospace>AMLGAM</monospace>,<sup><xref rid="ref23" ref-type="bibr">23</xref></sup><monospace>RXNMapper</monospace>,<sup><xref rid="ref24" ref-type="bibr">24</xref></sup><monospace>GraphormerMapper</monospace>,<sup><xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref></sup> and <monospace>LocalMapper</monospace>.<sup><xref rid="ref26" ref-type="bibr">26</xref></sup> These methods
bypass the formulation of the task as a combinatorial optimization
problem and avoid the computationally expensive subgraph matching
process by utilizing data-driven methods. Despite significant progress,
AAMs obtained from machine learning approaches are not perfect either,
as demonstrated both by recent benchmarking efforts<sup><xref rid="ref21" ref-type="bibr">21</xref></sup> and the performance data provided in the publications that
describe the individual tools.</p><p>The comparison of AAM predictions
generated by different tools
is not trivial. One practical issue is that each tool describes AAMs
using its own atom numbering. It was only proved recently that the
equivalence of AAMs could be reduced to isomorphism of certain auxiliary
graphs,<sup><xref rid="ref27" ref-type="bibr">27</xref></sup> notably including the Condensed
Graph of a Reaction (CGR),<sup><xref rid="ref28" ref-type="bibr">28</xref></sup> which was
introduced much earlier under the name <italic>Imaginary Transition
State</italic> (ITS) as a graph theoretical description of a chemical
reaction.<sup><xref rid="ref29" ref-type="bibr">29</xref></sup> The ITS graph provides a faithful
representation of a chemical reaction and thus contains the necessary
information to identify the reaction center. More precisely, the reaction
center corresponds to a subgraph of the ITS containing at least all
atoms incident to bonds that change during the transition from reactants
to products.<sup><xref rid="ref30" ref-type="bibr">30</xref></sup> These subgraphs of the ITS
are equivalent to the Double Pushout (DPO) graph rewriting rules used
by the <monospace>MØD</monospace> package.<sup><xref rid="ref6" ref-type="bibr">6</xref></sup> Reaction centers embedded in ITS graphs therefore provide
a source of reaction rules that can be used directly in large-scale
computational applications. A final technical challenge arises from
the fact that the comparison of AAMs requires that they are completely
specified.<sup><xref rid="ref27" ref-type="bibr">27</xref></sup> This is only possible if the
reaction data entry is balanced, i.e., if all participating molecules
are represented on both sides of the reaction. For the majority of
the data in the major repositories, however, this is not the case. <monospace>SynTemp</monospace> therefore greatly profits from recent advances
in reaction rebalancing.<sup><xref rid="ref31" ref-type="bibr">31</xref></sup></p><p>The <monospace>SynTemp</monospace> framework, introduced in this
paper, utilizes the equivalence of ITS graphs and AAMs, as well as
the fact that reaction patterns turn out to be equivalent to subgraphs
of the ITS graph, as the basis for its chemical reaction modeling.
The purpose of <monospace>SynTemp</monospace> is to extract a limited
number of explicit reaction rules from a large collection of reaction
data. In the following section, we describe in detail how this is
achieved. Two key ingredients are (1) to consider a hierarchy of partial
ITS graphs that contain the reaction centers as well as additional
structural contexts of different sizes and (2) to leverage clustering
to extract reaction patterns that are consistently observed in a large
collection of reaction data.</p><p>Other methods for template extraction,
such as <monospace>RDChiral</monospace>(<xref rid="ref32" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>) which employs SMARTS strings, have
been proposed. These methods, however, usually ignore critical components,
in particular hydrogen atoms and byproducts. In contrast, <monospace>SynTemp</monospace> incorporates these elements and thus provides
a more comprehensive and accurate framework for understanding and
modeling reaction mechanisms. Moreover, <monospace>SynTemp</monospace> utilizes an explicit graph representation instead of operating on
strings and thus completely avoids the well-known problems associated
with such linear encodings.<sup><xref rid="ref33" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>−<xref rid="ref35" ref-type="bibr">35</xref></sup></p></sec><sec id="sec2" disp-level="1"><label>2.</label><title>Theory and Methods</title><sec id="sec2.1" disp-level="2"><label>2.1.</label><title>General Framework</title><p>The notation adopted
in this study is derived from previous studies.<sup><xref rid="ref27" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>,<xref rid="ref36" ref-type="bibr">36</xref></sup> In order to make the presentation easier to read, we relegate the
full mathematical details to <ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.jcim.4c01795/suppl_file/ci4c01795_si_001.pdf" ext-link-type="uri">Supporting Section A.1</ext-link> and restrict ourselves here to a high-level description
of the framework.</p><p><monospace>SynTemp</monospace> operates through
four main stages, as outlined in <xref rid="fig1" ref-type="fig">Figure <xref rid="fig1" ref-type="fig">1</xref></xref>. The process initiates with a chemical reaction
input in SMILES format, from which an AAM is inferred. Formally, we
treat a chemical reaction as a map between two graphs <italic>G</italic> and <italic>H</italic> whose connected components are the reactant
and product molecules, respectively. The AAM is simply an invertible
map α : <italic>V</italic>(G) → <italic>V</italic>(<italic>H</italic>) between their vertex sets that preserves the atom types.
A reaction, therefore, corresponds to a rearrangement of edges, i.e.,
of chemical bonds. As discussed in the introduction, no perfect solution
exists for this task. We therefore leverage the agreement of multiple
state-of-the-art AAM tools (<monospace>RXNMapper</monospace>, <monospace>GraphormerMapper</monospace>, <monospace>LocalMapper</monospace>, <monospace>RDT</monospace>) to determine trustworthy AAMs. Comparison of AAMs
utilizes the theoretical framework developed in <xref rid="sec2.3" ref-type="sec">Section <xref rid="sec2.3" ref-type="sec">2.3</xref></xref> as well as ensemble learning.
Experimental procedures for the latter are described in more detail
in <ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.jcim.4c01795/suppl_file/ci4c01795_si_001.pdf" ext-link-type="uri">Supporting Section A.2</ext-link>.</p><fig id="fig1" position="float"><?disp-level 3?><label>Figure 1</label><caption><p>Procedure for extracting
reaction rules in <monospace>SynTemp</monospace>. (A) Reaction with
accurate atom–atom mapping between reactants
and products. (B) Complete ITS graph. (C) Extracted reaction center.
(D) Hierarchical clustering around the reaction center. Red edges
signify breaking bonds; green edges show forming bonds.</p></caption><alternatives><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" content-type="image" id="gr1" xlink:href="ci4c01795_0001.jpg"><?cloudpmc-path blobs/bd82/11938280/9f1750a1dd75/ci4c01795_0001.jpg?><?cloudpmc-bucket cdn?><?image-server-status LOAD_COMPLETED?><?original-height 799?><?original-width 840?><?scaled-height 3329?><?scaled-width 3500?></graphic><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" content-type="thumb" xlink:href="ci4c01795_0001.gif"><?cloudpmc-path blobs/bd82/11938280/00372dc113cb/ci4c01795_0001.gif?><?cloudpmc-bucket cdn?></graphic></alternatives></fig><p>Once the AAM is defined, it dictates the construction
of the ITS
graph Υ(<italic>G</italic>,<italic>H</italic>,α), which
combines the reactant and product structures. The vertices of <italic>G</italic> and <italic>H</italic> are identified in accordance with
the bijection α and the edge set of the ITS graph contains the
edges of both the reactant and product graphs <italic>G</italic> and <italic>H</italic>. Edge labels in the ITS graph distinguish between bonds
that are modified or deleted from reactants, bonds introduced in products,
or altered in bond type, and bonds that remain unchanged between <italic>G</italic> and <italic>H</italic>, see <xref rid="fig1" ref-type="fig">Figure <xref rid="fig1" ref-type="fig">1</xref></xref>B. An important practical complication is
introduced by the fact that AAM tools typically do not map hydrogen
atoms. However, the mechanisms of many important reactions involve
the forming and breaking of bonds to hydrogen atoms. This situation
can result in either a complete or partial ITS. In the case of a partial
ITS, implicit hydrogens are inferred to accurately depict the reaction
mechanism, as further elaborated on in <xref rid="sec2.4.1" ref-type="sec">Section <xref rid="sec2.4.1" ref-type="sec">2.4.1</xref></xref>.</p><p>The next phase involves identifying
the reaction center within
the ITS, consisting of the modified bonds and their adjacent atoms,
and capturing the transformation core. However, this minimal reaction
center alone is insufficient to describe all the preconditions necessary
for the reaction. Thus, we extend this subgraph by adding additional
vertices and bonds to obtain <italic>extended reaction centers</italic> as partial ITS graphs encoding the reaction rule, as discussed in <xref rid="sec2.4.2" ref-type="sec">Section <xref rid="sec2.4.2" ref-type="sec">2.4.2</xref></xref>.</p><p>Finally, these extended reaction centers are systematically classified
into clusters using a hierarchical clustering approach. We utilize
hierarchical clustering because it leads to a significant reduction
of the computational cost associated with the iterative isomorphism
checks required in graph clustering. By confining isomorphism checks
to subgroups within the same reaction center, hierarchical clustering
enhances the efficiency as the reaction center expands. For the details
of this methodology, see <xref rid="sec2.5" ref-type="sec">Section <xref rid="sec2.5" ref-type="sec">2.5</xref></xref>. The resulting clusters form a library of template
ITS graphs, which are converted into DPO rules in GML format for graph
transformation systems such as <monospace>MØD</monospace>. The
efficacy of these reaction rules is comprehensively evaluated in <xref rid="sec2.6" ref-type="sec">Section <xref rid="sec2.6" ref-type="sec">2.6</xref></xref>.</p></sec><sec id="sec2.2" disp-level="2"><label>2.2.</label><title>DPO Graph Rewriting and ITS Graphs</title><p>Chemical reactions can be modeled and studied through systems of
rule-based rewriting of molecular graphs.<sup><xref rid="ref7" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="ref37" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>,<xref rid="ref38" ref-type="bibr">38</xref></sup> These graph transformation systems require
(1) rules that prescribe how a pattern in the input graph is to be
changed within a larger substrate graph and (2) a definition of a
pattern match. In DPO graph rewriting,<sup><xref rid="ref39" ref-type="bibr">39</xref></sup> both facets are expressed in terms of morphisms, i.e., maps, between
graphs, providing a sound mathematical foundation in category theory.
A rule is specified as a so-called span, <inline-formula id="d34e522"><inline-graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="ci4c01795_m001.gif"><?cloudpmc-path blobs/bd82/11938280/a226c20b5344/ci4c01795_m001.gif?><?cloudpmc-bucket cdn?><?image-server-status NEVER_LOAD?><?scaled-height 46?><?scaled-width 200?></inline-graphic></inline-formula>, where <italic>L</italic>, <italic>K</italic>, and <italic>R</italic> are graphs and the arrows represent maps <italic>l</italic> : <italic>V</italic>(<italic>K</italic>) → <italic>V</italic>(<italic>L</italic>) and <italic>r</italic> : <italic>V</italic>(<italic>K</italic>) → <italic>V</italic>(<italic>R</italic>) which describe the embedding of <italic>K</italic> into <italic>L</italic> and <italic>R</italic> as a subgraph. The graph <italic>L</italic> describes the pattern in the reactants that is transformed
into a product pattern <italic>R</italic>. The “context graph” <italic>K</italic> and the maps <italic>l</italic> and <italic>r</italic> establish the correspondence of vertices and edges that remain unchanged
during the transformation from <italic>L</italic> to <italic>R</italic>. Since atoms do not change in the course of a chemical reaction,
the maps <italic>l</italic> and <italic>r</italic> together define
the AAM of the reaction. The edges of <italic>E</italic>(<italic>K</italic>) specify the chemical bonds that remain unchanged during the reaction,
i.e., <italic>xy</italic> ∈ <italic>E</italic>(<italic>K</italic>) implies <italic>l</italic>(<italic>xy</italic>) ∈ <italic>E</italic>(<italic>L</italic>) and <italic>r</italic>(<italic>xy</italic>) ∈ <italic>E</italic>(<italic>R</italic>). From a chemical
point of view, therefore, the DPO rule is a condensed representation
of the reaction mechanism. It specifies which bonds change, and how,
in the local vicinity of the reaction center. It is more general than
the reaction center because the context graph <italic>K</italic> can
also contain atoms and bonds that are not part of the reaction center.</p><p>Application of a rule <inline-formula id="d34e612"><inline-graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="ci4c01795_m002.gif"><?cloudpmc-path blobs/bd82/11938280/a226c20b5344/ci4c01795_m002.gif?><?cloudpmc-bucket cdn?><?image-server-status NEVER_LOAD?><?scaled-height 46?><?scaled-width 200?></inline-graphic></inline-formula> to a substrate <italic>G</italic> amounts
to finding a position of the pattern <italic>L</italic> in the graph <italic>G</italic> and then replacing <italic>L</italic> by <italic>R</italic> at this position. This yields the graph <italic>H</italic> representing
the reaction products. A graphical summary is presented in <xref rid="fig2" ref-type="fig">Figure <xref rid="fig2" ref-type="fig">2</xref></xref>. For a detailed
mathematical explanation of the application of rules, we refer to
the <ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.jcim.4c01795/suppl_file/ci4c01795_si_001.pdf" ext-link-type="uri">Supporting Section B</ext-link>.</p><fig id="fig2" position="float"><?disp-level 3?><label>Figure 2</label><caption><p>Illustration of the DPO
graph rewriting technique. This diagram

reaction rules crucially depends on the accuracy of the ITS and thus
on the underlying AAMs. While recent machine learning-based methods
have been reported to yield improvement over older methods which solve
combinatorial optimization problems, they are still subject to a non-negligible
degree of uncertainty which may result in unequivalent AAMs and very
different reaction centers. <xref rid="fig3" ref-type="fig">Figure <xref rid="fig3" ref-type="fig">3</xref></xref> shows an example of a reaction for which the results

(C) <monospace>LocalMapper</monospace> suggest a reaction center with a single cycle of size six.</p></caption><alternatives><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" content-type="image" id="gr3" xlink:href="ci4c01795_0003.jpg"><?cloudpmc-path blobs/bd82/11938280/259cdca36d26/ci4c01795_0003.jpg?><?cloudpmc-bucket cdn?><?image-server-status LOAD_COMPLETED?><?original-height 485?><?original-width 1002?><?scaled-height 2021?><?scaled-width 4175?></graphic><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" content-type="thumb" xlink:href="ci4c01795_0003.gif"><?cloudpmc-path blobs/bd82/11938280/55d85c96cfb5/ci4c01795_0003.gif?><?cloudpmc-bucket cdn?></graphic></alternatives></fig><p>The comparison of AAMs produced by two different
tools is a nontrivial
task. The reason is that each tool describes reactants and products
in its own way. The same reaction thus appears as α : <italic>V</italic>(<italic>G</italic>) → <italic>V</italic>(<italic>H</italic>) for one tool and as β : <italic>V</italic>(<italic>G</italic>′) → <italic>V</italic>(<italic>H</italic>′) for another. For both, we can construct the ITS graphs
Υ(<italic>G</italic>, <italic>H</italic>, α) and Υ(<italic>G</italic>′, <italic>H</italic>′, β), as well
as the reaction center subgraphs Γ(<italic>G</italic>, <italic>H</italic>′, α) and Γ(<italic>G</italic>′, <italic>H</italic>′, β), as described in <xref rid="sec2.4" ref-type="sec">Section <xref rid="sec2.4" ref-type="sec">2.4</xref></xref>. The AAMs are equivalent
if and only if the ITS graphs are isomorphic.<sup><xref rid="ref40" ref-type="bibr">40</xref></sup> In scenarios where reactions are balanced with complete atom–atom
maps, the isomorphism between Υ(<italic>G</italic>, <italic>H</italic>, α) and Υ(<italic>G</italic>′, <italic>H</italic>′, β) can be evaluated, for example using
the VF2 algorithm<sup><xref rid="ref41" ref-type="bibr">41</xref></sup> implemented in <monospace>NetworkX</monospace>, in order to determine their equivalence.</p><p>The situation is more complicated if a tool produces only a partial
AAM, which in particular is the case for unbalanced reactions. A formal
framework for partial AAMs has been described in our previous work.<sup><xref rid="ref36" ref-type="bibr">36</xref></sup> In general, the comparison of partial AAMs is
a difficult problem. We therefore consider here only the special case
that the AAMs cover the reaction center, i.e., that partial AAMs are
“good” in the sense of Def. Six in our previous work.<sup><xref rid="ref36" ref-type="bibr">36</xref></sup> Some mathematical results that are directly
relevant to our discussion are provided in <ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.jcim.4c01795/suppl_file/ci4c01795_si_001.pdf" ext-link-type="uri">Supporting Section A.2.1</ext-link> since the complete formal statements require
extensive notation borrowed from our previous work that we refrain
from introducing in the main text for ease of presentation.</p><p>In cases of incomplete atom–atom mappings, the <italic>sets</italic> of atoms mapped by different tools will in general also be different
and may vary substantially. This is in particular the case for tools
like <monospace>LocalMapper</monospace> that focus primarily on the
reaction center. The consistency of partial AAMs is formally defined
in Def. Nine of our previous work.<sup><xref rid="ref36" ref-type="bibr">36</xref></sup> Here
we will be content with a much simpler condition:</p><p>For two different
reactions <italic>G</italic> → <italic>H</italic> and <italic>G</italic>′ → <italic>H</italic>′, the idea
from<sup><xref rid="ref27" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>,<xref rid="ref36" ref-type="bibr">36</xref></sup> can be used to compare <italic>extended
reaction centers</italic>. We define these as subgraphs
Ψ and Ψ′ of the ITS graphs Υ(<italic>G</italic>, <italic>H</italic>, α) and Υ(<italic>G</italic>′, <italic>H</italic>′, β) that contain the reaction centers Γ(<italic>G</italic>, <italic>H</italic>, α) and Γ(<italic>G</italic>′, <italic>H</italic>′, β) as well as all vertices
in both reactant and product graphs within a distance at most <italic>r</italic> from a vertex in the reaction centers. If the two (complete)
AAMs α and β are consistent, then Ψ and Ψ′
must be isomorphic (for proof of this statement, see Proposition 3
in <ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.jcim.4c01795/suppl_file/ci4c01795_si_001.pdf" ext-link-type="uri">Supporting Section A.2.1</ext-link>). It is important
to note that the converse is <italic>not</italic> true, i.e., isomorphism
of extended reaction centers does not imply consistency of the reaction
maps. Counterexamples such as the one in <xref rid="fig9" ref-type="fig">Figure <xref rid="fig9" ref-type="fig">9</xref></xref> of our previous work,<sup><xref rid="ref36" ref-type="bibr">36</xref></sup> however, turn out to be very rare for chemical reaction
data. We therefore use Ψ ≅ Ψ′ as an efficiently
testable condition. For the comparison of AAMs, we employed this <italic>approximate condition</italic>, as detailed in <ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.jcim.4c01795/suppl_file/ci4c01795_si_001.pdf" ext-link-type="uri">Supporting Section A.2.2.</ext-link> This condition for AAMs comparison
facilitated the development of ensemble atom mappings.</p><fig id="fig9" position="float"><?disp-level 3?><label>Figure 9</label><caption><p>Cycle length analysis for <italic>Elementary</italic> reactions

(partial) AAM α, a (partial) ITS is readily constructed in linear
time. In practice, one can start with an edgeless graph comprising
the vertices of <italic>G</italic> endowed with the double labels
(<italic>a</italic><sub><italic>G</italic></sub>(<italic>x</italic>), <italic>a</italic><sub><italic>H</italic></sub>(α(<italic>x</italic>))) for all <italic>x</italic> ∈ <italic>V</italic>(<italic>G</italic>) and insert edges by iterating over the edge

recording the edge labels—see also.<sup><xref rid="ref27" ref-type="bibr">27</xref></sup> In the same manner, the reaction center Γ can be obtained
directly from the (partial) AAM restricted to the reaction vertices,
inserting reaction edges only.</p><sec id="sec2.4.1" disp-level="3"><label>2.4.1.</label><title>Completing the Reaction Center</title><p>Computational tools may return only partial AAMs, in particular ones
which do not represent hydrogen atoms. While hydrogens can usually
be suppressed safely in structural formula because simple valency
rules imply the missing hydrogens, this is no longer true for subgraphs
of the ITS. In particular, if only the reaction center extracted from
a partial AAM is known, the reattachment of hydrogens cannot be unambiguously
determined. As a consequence, without a complete representation of
the reaction mechanism, deriving comprehensive mechanistic insights
from “hydrogen-deficient” fragments of the ITS becomes
challenging. Moreover, hydrogens that take part in the reaction must
be present in reactant molecules, necessitating their inclusion in
the reaction rules of a chemical rewriting system such as <monospace>MØD</monospace>. We therefore strive to extract the most information-rich
representation of the ITS patterns.</p><p>Consider the example in <xref rid="fig4" ref-type="fig">Figure <xref rid="fig4" ref-type="fig">4</xref></xref>.1. Here, either
hydrogen atom H<sup>1</sup> or H<sup>2</sup> is transferred to the
hydroxy group. From a chemical point of view, hydrogen atoms H<sup>1</sup> and H<sup>2</sup> are equivalent, even though this equivalence
is not readily apparent to computational methods tasked with inserting
hydrogens. Such methods may yield two alternative reaction centers
depicted in <xref rid="fig4" ref-type="fig">Figure <xref rid="fig4" ref-type="fig">4</xref></xref>.1A,B. In this case, these graphs are isomorphic, indicating that
the reaction centers in A and B are interchangeable. The reaction
in <xref rid="fig4" ref-type="fig">Figure <xref rid="fig4" ref-type="fig">4</xref></xref>.2 shows,
however, that there is not always a unique hydrogen completion of
the ITS. In this particular example, the resulting reaction center
graphs, <xref rid="fig4" ref-type="fig">Figure <xref rid="fig4" ref-type="fig">4</xref></xref>.2A,B,
are not isomorphic, highlighting the complexity and ambiguity inherent
in achieving hydrogen completeness.</p><fig id="fig4" position="float"><?disp-level 4?><label>Figure 4</label><caption><p>First row shows an unambiguous hydrogen

be possible. The insertion of hydrogen can thus be regarded as a special
case within the extension of the reaction center, which is detailed
in <xref rid="sec2.4.2" ref-type="sec">Section <xref rid="sec2.4.2" ref-type="sec">2.4.2</xref></xref>. hydrogens as part of the reaction center. If the changes in Υ
involve a single pair of hydrogens, there exists a unique solution.
However, if multiple pairs are involved, the situation escalates in
complexity, yielding a number of combinations that grows exponentially
with the number of pairs of hydrogens that undergo change. In cases
where multiple potential solutions exist, we handle hydrogen reattachment
by considering multiple AAMs in the subsequent analysis steps. If
all AAMs α are identical, or if all extended ITS graphs Υ(<italic>G</italic>′, <italic>H</italic>′, α′)

ITS.</p><p>Hydrogen atoms may furthermore be problematic by originating
from
environmental sources such as solvents. Ideally, this would be explicit
in the reaction data that we use as input. Usually, however, this
is not the case. To manage this, we decompose reactions into sequences
that account for “borrowed” environmental hydrogens.
For a more detailed discussion, we refer to our recent study.<sup><xref rid="ref36" ref-type="bibr">36</xref></sup> In <monospace>SynTemp</monospace>, we initially
exclude external hydrogen atom in the construction of the ITS database
and add relevant hydrogen later for each mechanistic step.</p></sec><sec id="sec2.4.2" disp-level="3"><label>2.4.2.</label><title>Extended Reaction Centers</title><p>Reaction
mechanisms are oftentimes not only influenced by reaction vertices
and edges but also by adjacent structural components such as functional
groups that determine the local chemical environment. It is desirable,
therefore, to consider patterns <italic>L</italic> in DPO rules that
also include features that are not altered during the reaction. Formally
this can be achieved by considering subgraphs <italic>Q</italic> of
the ITS Υ(<italic>G</italic>, <italic>H</italic>, α) containing
the reaction center as a proper subgraph, Γ ⊂ <italic>Q</italic> ⊆ Υ(<italic>G</italic>, <italic>H</italic>, α). The choice of the graph <italic>Q</italic> is the difficult
issue here.</p><p>It stands to reason that the necessary context around
the reaction center cannot be determined from a single reaction. Such
information can be inferred by considering a set of reactions with
the same reaction mechanism. A (mechanistically defined) reaction
type or <italic>named reaction</italic>, such as the Diels–Alder
reaction in <xref rid="fig1" ref-type="fig">Figure <xref rid="fig1" ref-type="fig">1</xref></xref>C above, however, is usually not annotated in most reaction data
records. cost.</p></sec><sec id="sec2.6" disp-level="2"><label>2.6.</label><title>Application of Reaction Rules</title><p>The
final result of the <monospace>SynTemp</monospace> pipeline is a collection
of partial ITS graphs which describe reaction rules at different resolutions
depending on the extent to which the reaction centers have been expanded.
We refer to these partial ITS graphs as <italic>reaction templates</italic>, while reserving the term <italic>reaction rules</italic> for encoding
of these data as DPO rewriting rules.</p><p>The template graphs are
transformed into DPO rules, encoded in GML format for direct use with <monospace>MØD</monospace>. In order to check the validity and usefulness
of the extracted rules, we apply a rule to a set of reactants of a
known reaction and check whether the application of the graph rewriting
rule recovers the expected products. ITS graphs also implicitly encode
the reverse of a reaction. To this end, it suffices to exchange the
first and second entry in the tuple of edge labels. Making use of
this symmetry, we also tested whether the application of the reverse
rule to a set of product molecules could recover the reactants.</p><p>The performance of rule application is quantified as the <italic>coverage</italic><inline-formula id="d34e1142"><inline-graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="ci4c01795_m016.gif"><?cloudpmc-path blobs/bd82/11938280/2db7f7abbf33/ci4c01795_m016.gif?><?cloudpmc-bucket cdn?><?image-server-status NEVER_LOAD?><?scaled-height 11?><?scaled-width 8?></inline-graphic></inline-formula>, defined as the fraction of reactions correctly
recovered by applying a rule to a set of reactants. However, in many
cases, templates also match with a set of reactants other than the
one from the rule extracted. In these cases, we obtain predictions
for novel reactions. We quantify this as the <italic>novelty rate</italic><inline-formula id="d34e1146"><inline-graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="ci4c01795_m017.gif"><?cloudpmc-path blobs/bd82/11938280/db93aeb19c69/ci4c01795_m017.gif?><?cloudpmc-bucket cdn?><?image-server-status NEVER_LOAD?><?scaled-height 11?><?scaled-width 18?></inline-graphic></inline-formula>, defined as the fraction of successful
applications of the rule that results in a novel reaction.</p></sec><sec id="sec2.7" disp-level="2"><label>2.7.</label><title>Dataset</title><p>We compare AAM methods and
ensemble learning techniques using two subsets. Their reaction type

compounds.</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec3" disp-level="1"><label>3.</label><title>Computational Results</title><p>In this section,

presents <monospace>SynTemp</monospace>, a framework
for automatic reaction template/rule extraction. <monospace>SynTemp</monospace> supplies reaction rules as ITS subgraphs, equivalent to DPO graph
rewriting rules, for immediate application to new substrates via <monospace>M</monospace>ØD. The graphs contain the reaction center as a
subgraph and thus describe a classification of reaction that refines
e.g., the classification proposed in ref (<xref rid="ref42" ref-type="bibr">42</xref>). <monospace>SynTemp</monospace> successfully
tackles key technical problems associated with the inference of interpretable
reaction patterns, namely the large-scale computation of reliable
AAMs, the comparison of AAMs based on information, and the efficient
clustering of pattern graphs with varying detail levels beyond the
reaction center.</p><p>The present implementation uses an approximation
for AAMs comparison
on partial ITS graphs that delivers accuracies well above 90% on benchmark
sets, but may occasionally return false positives. Computationally
inferred AAMs form the basis of our approach to extract reaction templates.
To this end, we integrated multiple state-of-the-art AAM tools to
enhance accuracy. Specifically, we combined <monospace>RXNMapper</monospace>, <monospace>GraphormerMapper</monospace>, and <monospace>LocalMapper</monospace>, achieving an accuracy of 99.47% with a success rate of 71.23% on
the <italic>Chemical Reaction Dataset</italic>. The ensemble approach
of extracted templates to atom–atom mapping exhibits high confidence
in correctly representing the underlying chemical mechanisms.</p><p>In the absence of extensive collections of reaction rules against
which our results could be compared, we opted to maximize accuracy
and confidence in the rule sets at the expense of <italic>coverage</italic>. We ignore nonequivalent AAMs and ambiguous hydrogens, which account
for approximately 14% of the database. The <italic>coverage</italic> results in rule application within radii zero being limited to 93.5–94.5%,
which is only slightly better than the 93.3% <italic>coverage</italic> achieved by <monospace>RDChiral</monospace> using SMARTS templates.
However, this is achieved by a library of reaction patterns that,
at present, contains only 311 entries, compared to more than 10,000
SMARTS, indicating a drastic difference in generalization ability.</p><p>In the present implementation, the context around the reaction
center is simply defined by an expansion radius, i.e., it includes
all atoms within a given graph-theoretical distance. Refining this
context part of the rules remains an interesting research question.
One promising approach is graph alignments, for which methods have
recently become available.<sup><xref rid="ref40" ref-type="bibr">40</xref></sup> These could
be used to distinguish common from variable parts of the context.</p><p><monospace>SynTemp</monospace> can efficiently process 50,000 reactions
within minutes on personal hardware. The effectiveness of <monospace>SynTemp</monospace> – and all similar tools–heavily
depends on the accuracy and efficiency of the AAM inference process.
The performance is therefore tied to the quality of the reaction data
that are used as input. Unbalanced reactions in general compromise
the reliability of results and complicate the inference process. The
success rate of <monospace>SynRBL</monospace> in imputing missing
compounds in the <monospace>Reaxys</monospace> database is approximately
76%.<sup><xref rid="ref31" ref-type="bibr">31</xref></sup> Reaction patterns inferred by <monospace>SynTemp</monospace> may contribute to future improvements of tools
for correcting reaction data since they can help improve the coverage
of reaction types in machine learning classifiers<sup><xref rid="ref51" ref-type="bibr">51</xref>,<xref rid="ref52" ref-type="bibr">52</xref></sup> and improve the voting process among AAM tools with new methods
such as the cycle descriptor for classifying reaction center topologies.</p><p>Given the prevalence of multistep reactions in the dataset, <monospace>SynTemp</monospace> leverages <monospace>MØD</monospace>’s
rule composition features to explain these reactions as constituting
single-step components.<sup><xref rid="ref50" ref-type="bibr">50</xref>,<xref rid="ref53" ref-type="bibr">53</xref></sup> Reactions involving
fewer steps are generally more chemically plausible, suggesting lower
energy barriers and streamlined processes. This adds credibility to
combinatorial AAM mapping approaches that seek reaction centers composed
of few cycles, as advocated in the study of Mann et al.<sup><xref rid="ref54" ref-type="bibr">54</xref></sup> Nevertheless, there is no guarantee that the
true mechanism minimizes descriptor lengths. Complex mechanisms may
also form bonds that are broken again in subsequent steps and thus
do not appear at all in the ITS and reaction center graphs. To address
this issue at least in part, overlay graphs that extend the ITS have
been introduced by.<sup><xref rid="ref55" ref-type="bibr">55</xref></sup> These graphs detail
both the imaginary transition states and transient bonds within multistep
reactions. Integrating this with recent insights into partial ITS
graphs<sup><xref rid="ref36" ref-type="bibr">36</xref></sup> could significantly advance both
our understanding and computational capabilities of multistep reaction
mechanisms. Notably, a formal framework for rule composition is absent
in SMARTS, highlighting the benefits of explicit graph transformations
for modeling reactions. Finally, the correct and unambiguous completion
of hydrogens in the ITS graphs, as a special issue in the context
of AAM inference, also remains a topic for future improvements.</p></sec><sec id="ack1" sec-type="ack" disp-level="1"><title>Acknowledgments</title><p>This project has received funding from the European
